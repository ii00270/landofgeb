01  —  Why this is different

Two things kill a job.
We designed against both.

Schedule slip and scope dispute. Nearly every troubled contract traces back to one of them, and both are usually written into the quote before work begins — because the price was named before the work was understood.

Against schedule slip

A gate that refuses the job.

Estimated work is measured against the delivery window at scoping. If the critical path alone cannot fit, we decline to quote and say what it would actually take — more people, or more time. A contractor who will tell you no before the award is a contractor who won't be telling you why they're late after it.

Against scope dispute

Deliverables that are binary.

Every deliverable is defined before work starts — done or not done, no partial credit, no interpretation. The argument that normally happens at closeout happens at scoping instead, when it costs nothing and changes everything.

And the books prove themselves

Before any cycle closes, an arithmetic audit confirms that labor, reserve, and remainder sum exactly to gross. Identifiers carry mod-97 check digits that reject a transposed character before it reaches a record. A sealed cycle is immutable — the record of what happened cannot be quietly edited afterward.

Every one of these is a rule, not a promise. They're written into the network's constitution, they apply to every engagement identically, and they are not adjustable to win a price-sensitive client.
02  —  On a live task order

How the work
actually moves.

The same six moves as any other engagement — with two federal-specific checks added at the points where they belong.

01
Read the solicitation as a graph, not a list
The statement of work becomes a dependency-ordered map. What runs in parallel, what's blocked behind what, and where the real critical path sits — visible before a number exists.
02
Check the window
Estimated hours against the period of performance. If the critical path alone exceeds it, we don't bid and we say why. The refusal gate is not overridable by price.
03
Check the wage floor
On a Service Contract Act–covered engagement, each labor category is checked against the Department of Labor wage determination for that locality at bid time. The rate paid is the greater of the two.
04
Derive the price
Each unit of work multiplies by its owner's rate; the sum is grossed so the reserve is funded off the top. Firm-fixed-price, from a breakdown we can show you line by line on request.
05
Route the work by proven record
Work goes to members whose standing was computed from what they actually delivered — not from who they know. Coordination, messaging, and verification all happen inside the system, next to the work.
06
Close it, audit it, seal it
Deliverables close binary. The arithmetic reconciles to gross. The cycle seals immutable, and the record of the job becomes past performance that can be pointed at.
The wage floor
Member rate = max ( tier rate, SCA wage determination )
Checked once, at bid time, via the SAM.gov wage determination lookup. Where the determination is higher than our published tier rate, the determination governs — and every downstream number in the pricing formula flows from the corrected figure automatically.
03  —  What we bid

Firm-fixed-price,
from a real breakdown.

Our pricing method produces a derived, defensible number rather than a market guess — which makes firm-fixed-price the natural fit, and time-and-materials equally straightforward where a vehicle calls for it.

Firm-Fixed-Price

A number with a map behind it.

Every figure traces back to a defined unit of work with an owner and an hour estimate. If you want the derivation, we will walk you through it line by line.

Time & Materials

Published rates, applied plainly.

Where a vehicle is structured as T&M, our rates apply directly with no repackaging — including subcontract positions under an established prime, and task orders under an existing JOC or SABER vehicle.

06  —  Capacity

Scale without
losing the method.

A job larger than one operator doesn't get a looser process — it gets more of the same one. Work routes to members whose standing was computed from delivered results, at published rates, under deliverables defined the same binary way. The decomposition doesn't change because more hands are on it.

That's the point of building the network the way it's built: capability is a number computed from record, not a claim on a résumé. When a scope needs four people, the system already knows which four have actually delivered that kind of work — and the client sees one accountable counterparty, one flat price, and one audited settlement.
